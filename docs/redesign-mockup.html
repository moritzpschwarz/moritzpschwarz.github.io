I am an applied economist and econometrician working on causal identification, empirical macro-modelling, and environmental and climate policy.

I obtained my DPhil (PhD) in Economics from the University of Oxford working with the Climate Econometrics group at Nuffield College, and I am based at the Faculty of Economics and Management at TU Berlin and at the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research (PIK). I remain affiliated with the Institute for New Economic Thinking at the Oxford Martin School and the Smith School of Enterprise and the Environment.

Research

My research combines econometric method development with empirical macroeconomics and policy evaluation. It is organised around three strands:

  1. Causal identification and reverse-causal inference
    I develop tools to detect unknown interventions and regime shifts as structural breaks in panel and time-series models, linking break detection to modern causal-inference frameworks. These methods underpin applications such as identifying which national climate and energy policies have actually reduced emissions (e.g. work published in Science and Nature Energy) and improving robustness in climate-impact estimation (e.g. Journal of Econometrics).

  2. Empirical macro-modelling and forecasting under structural change
    I lead the development of the Open-Source Empirical Macro (OSEM) model, a multi-equation empirical framework that jointly forecasts macroeconomic, energy, and environmental variables. OSEM combines automatic model selection, break detection, and error-correction techniques and is used by Austria’s Ministry of Finance and the UK Department for Energy Security and Net Zero. This work complements structural/DSGE and CGE-type models and speaks to macro-fiscal, financial-stability, and transition questions.

  3. Applications in climate, energy, and just transitions
    I apply these methods to quantify the real-world impacts of climate change and climate policy, including the effectiveness of national policy packages, the macroeconomic consequences of warming and extremes, and the labour-market and distributional dimensions of the clean energy transition. This includes work on just transition, state-owned enterprises, and the interaction between fiscal policy and climate objectives.

Across these strands, a common theme is empirical transparency in macroeconomic and environmental policy analysis: using open, reproducible tools to understand which policies work, how shocks propagate through the economy, and how to design more resilient macroeconomic and climate strategies.

Research highlights

Discovering What Mattered: detecting unknown treatment

My job-market paper with Felix Pretis develops the econometric foundations for investigating changes whose timing and assignment are not known in advance. We connect structural breaks to heterogeneous treatment effects in fixed-effects panel models, linking treatment detection to conventional policy-evaluation designs. Applications revisit Sweden’s carbon tax and the economic effects of ETA terrorism. Detecting a break identifies a candidate intervention; attributing it to a particular cause still requires substantive investigation.

The paper is under review at the Journal of the American Statistical Association. Paper and abstract.

OSEM: empirical macroeconomic and emissions forecasting

I lead development of the Open-Source Empirical Macroeconomic (OSEM) framework, which jointly models economic activity, energy use and emissions. OSEM retains accounting relationships while estimating economic dynamics from observed data. Our paper presents the framework and evaluates its real-time out-of-sample forecasting performance. The framework is used by Austria’s Ministry of Finance and the UK Department for Energy Security and Net Zero, with these applications informing its continuing development.

Our paper is conditionally accepted at the International Journal of Forecasting. Publication details · OSEM documentation.

Temporary warming, lasting losses: climate overshoot

In joint work with S. Schöngart, J. Schwaab, F. Pretis, P. Pfleiderer and C.-F. Schleußner, we examine the economic consequences of temporarily exceeding a temperature target. The paper investigates whether the economic damages from climate overshoot may persist within this century, even when warming subsequently declines. It connects my work on empirical climate impacts with questions about the economic implications of different temperature pathways.

The paper is at revise and resubmit at One Earth. Publication details.

Science: Identifying successful climate policies

As part of an international research team, we have published the first global assessment of climate policies in the four most critical emission sectors in the journal Science.

We unveiled the first comprehensive global evaluation of 1,500 climate policy measures from 41 countries across six continents. We identified ‘emission breaks’ and assigned them policy interventions, pinpointing where policies may have had a large impact. The break detection methodology, called indicator saturation estimation, developed at Climate Econometrics, allows break indicators for all possible dates to be examined objectively using a variant of machine learning. This unprecedented study provides a detailed impact analysis of the wide range of climate policy measures implemented across the planet over the last two decades.

We have identified 63 cases of successful climate policies with large emission reductions with the key characteristic of these successful cases being the inclusion of tax and price incentives in well-designed policy mixes. Our study also shows that if more countries relied on policies like these ones, the remaining emissions gap for 2030 could be closed by as much as 26% to 41%. The identified successful policies have led to an average emission reduction of 19 percent. In total, the 63 policy interventions reduced emissions between 0.6 and 1.8 billion tonnes of CO2.

For more detail on the identified policies, check out our Climate Policy Explorer where you can dive deeper into our results and findings.

Stechemesser A., Koch, N., Mark E., Klösel P., Nachtigall, D., Pretis, F., Ritter N., Schwarz M., et al. (2024). Climate policies that achieved major emission reductions: Global evidence from two decades. Science.

Energy Policy: Labour Market Impacts of Coal Mine Closures

We have recently published a paper in Energy Policy that considers the dynamics of the US labour market that are associated with coal mines closing. The paper finds that coal mine closures raise US county unemployment rates with spatial ripple effects and that Just Transition interventions should tailor to local conditions to alleviate impacts.

Mark, E., Rafaty, R. and Schwarz, M. (2024). Spatio-temporal dynamics of structural unemployment in declining coal mining regions and potentialities of the ‘just transition’. Energy Policy.

Software

I contribute to several open-source R packages that implement the methods I use in my research:

  • {gets}: general-to-specific modelling, indicator saturation, structural breaks, and automated model selection.
  • {getspanel}: extension of gets-type methods to panel data and causal-inference settings (including reverse-causal identification).
  • {osem}: implementation of the Open-Source Empirical Macro model for joint macro–energy–emissions forecasting and scenario analysis.

These packages are widely used by researchers and policy institutions and form the backbone of my empirical work.

Teaching

I teach environmental economics, econometrics and statistics, from introductory empirical work to doctoral methods workshops. My teaching combines economic questions, explicit assumptions and practical analysis in R. Students work through the reasoning behind a method, implement it, and assess what the results can support.

My experience includes MSc seminars and lectures at Oxford, independently designed PhD workshops at TU Berlin, undergraduate and master’s teaching contributions, and training in applied data analysis for researchers and policy practitioners.

Doctoral methods workshops · TU Berlin

Causal Inference: Foundations, Methods and Frontiers

PhD level · Two-day workshop · Independently designed and taught

The workshop starts from potential outcomes and identification, then examines instrumental variables, difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity and synthetic controls. Discussion of treatment heterogeneity, staggered adoption and synthetic difference-in-differences connects established designs to more recent methods. Worked examples and R exercises focus on choosing a credible comparison, making assumptions explicit and interpreting estimates.

Course overview and learning outcomes →

Applied Time-Series Econometrics

PhD level · Two-day workshop · Independently designed and taught

The course develops empirical models of economic dynamics, from autoregressive behaviour and distributed lags to non-stationarity, cointegration and equilibrium correction. It then considers structural change, vector autoregressions and the identification of structural shocks. Practical work in R emphasises model adequacy, sensitivity and the distinction between statistical relationships and causal interpretation.

Environmental economics · Oxford

At Oxford, I designed and led MSc Environmental Economics seminar sessions as a teaching assistant, and delivered two lectures in New Environmental Economic Thinking. My teaching links environmental-policy questions to the empirical and modelling tools used to investigate them.

Applied R and data analysis

My Nottingham teaching materials cover an applied R workflow: importing and inspecting data, transforming and combining datasets with the tidyverse, visualising results with ggplot2, and estimating and presenting regression models. Exercises connect individual commands to a reproducible analysis, with attention to readable code and checking intermediate results.

Training materials for Austria’s Ministry of Finance introduce R and RStudio, data manipulation and project-based workflows through budget-data examples. The focus is on helping participants read, run and adapt an analysis, and understand how a script records the steps behind a result.

Supervision and prospective students

I have co-supervised undergraduate and master’s dissertations at TU Berlin and Oxford, and contributed to the supervision of empirical chapters by doctoral researchers. I help students develop a feasible question, choose an empirical design, work through the analysis and communicate its limitations.

Prospective students are welcome to email me about research interests in econometrics, environmental-policy evaluation, empirical macroeconomics and forecasting. A brief description of your interests, background and proposed question is helpful.
